When shoppers

Can shopping patterns help a retailer decide who to contact—and what to test before spending money on retention?

This project follows grocery purchases from changing baskets to departure risk, then explores the limits of a targeted offer.

01 / Observe

The basket changes.
What does that tell us?

Some categories lose a larger share of household spending around the transition to dormancy. Comparing these changes with consistently active shoppers gives us a signal to investigate.

How to read this

A clue about behavior,
not a reason for leaving.

The bars compare category shares across earlier and later three-period windows around first dormancy. The later window includes dormancy itself, so this analysis does not establish an eight-week advance warning.

What we cannot conclude

We see purchases at one chain. We cannot tell whether a shopper changed stores, changed habits, or stopped needing a product. These patterns need prospective testing before becoming alerts.

02 / Predict

Focus limited attention
where risk is concentrated.

A churn model estimates which households will make no shopping trips in the next two periods (56 days). Purchase history is available before that window; future outcomes are used to evaluate the predictions.

The practical finding

Risk is concentrated.
Response is still unknown.

Ranking households gives a retailer a more focused outreach list than contacting people at random. The next question is whether that outreach actually changes behavior.

A useful baseline

LightGBM’s PR-AUC is 0.380, compared with 0.320 for logistic regression. Their confidence intervals overlap. The more complex model has not established a clear advantage on that metric.

03 / Test

A prediction is a starting point.
An experiment is the next step.

Historical campaigns were not randomly assigned. Uplift models explore who might respond differently to an offer, but selection bias can remain. A randomized holdout is needed before claiming incremental profit or suppressing contact.

Design

Randomize households.

Choose eligibility in advance, then assign households to an offer or a holdout. Keep each household in one arm and record delivery costs.

Measure

Compare net outcomes.

Track spend or margin over a fixed follow-up window, together with offer cost. Choose the detectable effect and sample size before running the test.

Decide

Roll out on evidence.

Use the treatment-versus-holdout difference and its uncertainty. Check discount dependence and subgroup outcomes before expanding the policy.

Study planning: 5,340 households needed; 739 eligible households available. This proposal needs a larger study pool or a revised design before launch.

Calibration, temporal splits, error analysis, and limitations are documented in the technical report.

What the lifecycle model adds

Backtest distribution error (lower is better): Markov 0.0186; unchanged-distribution baseline 0.0125. Assumption checks also find evidence against a time-stable, first-order chain. Use the groups to describe activity; treat long-range forecasts cautiously.

The lifecycle model groups shopping activity into New, Champion, Regular, At-Risk, Dormant, and Lapsed states. A transition matrix describes how frequently households move between states in 28 days.

Long-range projections assume those transition patterns remain stable. Treating Lapsed as absorbing is a mathematical simplification: shoppers can return in real life. State-value estimates and 26-week household margin predictions use different horizons and should not be compared directly.

Data, reproducibility, and limits

The source contains transaction line items, households, products, campaign exposure, and coupon records. SQL/dbt models create baskets and household-period states; Python produces evaluations and saved outputs. This page is generated from those outputs.

  • Single-chain historical data cannot establish why someone leaves.
  • Campaign-household observations can repeat the same household. Unique households and campaign observations must be reported separately.
  • Estimated customer value and offer response introduce uncertainty into financial scenarios.
  • Small demographic groups limit subgroup comparisons; omitting demographics does not establish fairness.

The difference an intervention might make, compared with not intervening.

Whether predicted probabilities agree with observed frequencies.
